1. Why Finance Exists

At its core, finance solves a fundamental mismatch in society: Some people and institutions have excess money (surplus), while others have productive ideas or urgent needs but lack cash (deficit).

Without a financial system, a local entrepreneur who wants to build a factory would have to personally track down thousands of individual savers with spare cash. The financial system acts as the ultimate bridge, matching surplus capital with productive demand efficiently, securely, and at scale.

Real-Life Example: When you deposit €100 into a savings account, you are acting as a saver. The bank pools your money with thousands of others and lends it to a family buying a home or a startup creating new green energy jobs.

2. Key Vocabulary

Term Meaning
Financial system The network of institutions, markets and rules that moves money from those who have spare to those who need it.
Saver / Investor Someone with surplus money who wants it to grow.
Borrower / Issuer A person, company or government that needs money now.
Financial intermediary A go-between, such as a bank or mutual fund, that connects savers and borrowers.
Financial asset / security A tradable claim on future cash flows (e.g., stocks, bonds).
Liquidity How quickly an asset can be converted into cash without losing value.
Risk-return trade-off Higher potential return usually comes with higher risk of loss.
Buy-side vs. Sell-side Buy-side invests money (asset managers); sell-side sells services and products (investment banks, brokers).

3. Structure of the Financial System

A robust financial system relies on four foundational pillars:

  • Institutions: Commercial banks, credit unions, and insurance companies.
  • Markets: Stock exchanges (like NYSE or NASDAQ) and bond markets.
  • Instruments: Stocks, bonds, loans, and derivatives.
  • Regulators: Central banks (e.g., ECB, Federal Reserve) enforcing monetary rules and stability.

4. Savers & Borrowers (Future Value Math)

When money is lent or invested, it grows over time based on interest rates. The mathematical relationship governing future obligations is:

Future Value Formula

Future Value = Principal × (1 + Interest Rate)t

Where t is time periods (usually years).

5. What Is a Financial Intermediary?

Instead of individuals searching for borrowers directly, intermediaries pool funds and manage risk:

  • Commercial Banks: Take deposits from savers and issue mortgages or business loans.
  • Mutual Funds: Pool investors' money to buy diversified stock and bond portfolios.
  • Insurance Companies: Collect premiums and invest reserves until claims are filed.
  • Pension Funds: Gather employee retirement contributions for long-term compounding.

6. Financial Assets & Liquidity

A financial asset is a non-physical asset whose value stems from a contractual claim or ownership right. Unlike a physical car or house, it represents a legal promise of future cash flows.

Liquidity Spectrum:
  • High: Cash, savings accounts (instant access).
  • Medium: Public stocks and bonds (sold in seconds on exchanges, but settlement takes days).
  • Low: Real estate, private equity, physical art (takes months to convert to cash at fair market value).

7. Risk & The Risk-Return Trade-Off

Investors demand higher expected compensation when taking on higher uncertainty. This trade-off is often evaluated using the Sharpe Ratio:

Sharpe Ratio = (Expected Return – Risk-Free Rate) / Standard Deviation (Risk)

8. Stakeholders in Finance

Financial decisions impact multiple groups:

  • Internal Stakeholders: Employees, executives, and owners directly tied to operations.
  • External Stakeholders: Customers, suppliers, regulators, and society impacted by market outcomes.
