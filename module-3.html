1. Why is £100 today worth more than £100 next year?

If someone offers you £100 right now or £100 exactly 12 months from now, you should always take the £100 today. This core economic principle is known as the Time Value of Money (TVM). It is driven by three powerful forces:

  • Opportunity Cost: If you have £100 today, you can deposit it in a savings account or invest it to earn interest, meaning it will grow to be more than £100 next year.
  • Inflation: Prices generally rise over time, so £100 will buy fewer goods and services in the future than it does today.
  • Risk and Uncertainty: A promise of future money carries the risk that the borrower might default or that circumstances might change. Cash in hand is certain.
Real-Life Analogy: Think of a smartphone. If you buy it today, you enjoy using it for an entire year. If you wait a year to buy it with the same £100, inflation might have driven the smartphone's price up to £108, or you missed out on earning 5% interest in the bank.

2. Complete Vocabulary Glossary (17 Terms)

Term Meaning
Interest The price of borrowing money or the reward for lending it.
Interest rate Interest expressed as a percentage per period.
Simple vs. compound interest Interest on the original amount only vs. interest on the amount plus past interest ("interest on interest").
Principal The original amount borrowed or invested.
Present value (PV) What a future sum is worth today.
Future value (FV) What a sum today will be worth later.
Discount rate The rate used to convert future money into today's money.
Net present value (NPV) The sum of discounted future cash flows minus initial cost; positive NPV creates value.
Internal rate of return (IRR) The discount rate at which NPV equals zero.
Annuity A fixed payment at regular intervals.
Perpetuity An annuity that never ends.
Inflation The general rise in prices, which reduces purchasing power.
Real vs. nominal rate Real is adjusted for inflation; nominal is the headline rate.
Opportunity cost The value of the best alternative you give up.
Yield Income return on an investment, as a percentage of price.
Basis point (bp) 0.01%; 100 bps = 1%.
APR / APY (EAR) Annual percentage rate (without compounding) vs. annual percentage yield (with compounding).

3. Simple vs. Compound Interest

Simple interest calculates earnings only on the original principal amount. If you invest £1,000 at 5% simple interest for 3 years, you earn £50 each year (£150 total).

Compound interest calculates earnings on the principal plus all accumulated past interest ("interest on interest"). In year 2, you earn interest on £1,050, accelerating your wealth exponentially over time.

4. Core Mathematical Formulas

Future Value (FV)

FV = PV × (1 + r)t

Where PV is Present Value, r is interest rate per period, and t is number of periods.

Present Value (PV) / Discounting

PV = FV / (1 + r)t

Brings a future cash flow back to today's value using the discount rate r.

6. Net Present Value (NPV) & Internal Rate of Return (IRR)

When businesses evaluate projects or investments, they look at Net Present Value (NPV):

NPV = ∑ [ Cash Flowt / (1 + r)t ] - Initial Investment

If NPV is positive, the project adds value and should be accepted. The Internal Rate of Return (IRR) is the specific discount rate that forces NPV to exactly zero.

The Fisher Equation (Real vs. Nominal Rates):

Nominal interest rates include inflation, whereas real rates reflect actual purchasing power growth.

1 + Nominal Rate = (1 + Real Rate) × (1 + Inflation Rate)

8. APR vs. APY (Effective Annual Rate)

APR is the simple annual rate without compounding. APY (or EAR) accounts for compounding frequency within the year:

APY = (1 + r / n)n - 1

Where r is stated annual rate and n is compounding periods per year.
